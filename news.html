Free Chromebooks for Class 9 High Scorers — Prime Minister's Digital Pakistan Initiative

All principals and heads of government schools in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa are informed that Class 9 students who obtained 424 or more marks in the Science group or 315 or more marks in the Arts group (SSC Annual-I Examination 2026) are eligible for free Chromebooks under the Prime Minister's Digital Pakistan Initiative Programme.

BISE Peshawar has already submitted the data of eligible students to the Inter Boards Coordination Commission (IBCC), Islamabad. School heads must check the list of their institution's eligible students, fill in the official proforma with complete, accurate and verified information, and submit the duly signed hard copy to the Office of the Director Equivalence, IBCC, Plot No. 25, Street No. 38, G-10/4, Islamabad, with intimation to the Director Research & Development, BISE Peshawar.

Hackers Breach 1Password — Customer Data Stolen, But Password Vaults Stay Safe

One of the world’s most popular password managers has been hacked. 1Password, the app millions of people trust to store their passwords, confirmed on Wednesday that hackers broke into its internal computer systems and stole account information belonging to some of its customers. The good news, the company says, is that no passwords were taken. The vaults where customers keep their logins, bank details and private notes were never reached — and neither were the secret keys that protect them.

The company, based in Toronto, Canada, said it first noticed something wrong on 19 November, when it began investigating suspicious activity around an employee’s corporate account on GitHub, the website where programmers store and share their code. The attacker had gained access to that account and used it to reach 1Password’s private source code and to push malicious code into the company’s backend systems — the servers that run the service behind the scenes. That code then downloaded account records belonging to a small group of customers. 1Password has not said how many people were affected, describing the group only as a subset of its users.

The information the attacker copied includes customers’ names and the email addresses they use to sign in, along with account and user IDs, the dates accounts were created, and the times users last signed in. The attacker also took public cryptographic keys, some technical details used in the sign-in process, and tokens hidden inside emailed links that 1Password sends when someone is invited to join an account or needs to recover one. The attacker also copied some of the company’s private source code and reached its software build system, which could have exposed internal credentials. 1Password says it has now disabled the compromised GitHub account, revoked the stolen token, restored clean versions of the affected services, and rotated or revoked any internal credentials that may have been exposed.

What the attacker did not get is the part that matters most. No customer vault content was accessed — no passwords, no credit card numbers, no secure notes and no other items stored inside vaults. The encryption keys that protect those vaults, the account recovery keys, and customers’ own account passwords and Secret Keys were also untouched. The reason is the way 1Password is built. The service uses end-to-end encryption, which means the keys needed to read what is stored inside a vault are held only by the user, not by the company. Even when someone breaks into 1Password’s own servers, the contents of the vaults stay locked. “Your vault secrets are encrypted with keys that only you hold, so we can’t access them, and neither can an attacker who gets into our servers,” the company said. The official apps and browser extensions were not affected either.

So if no passwords were stolen, why should users care? Security experts say the stolen data is still valuable to criminals — because it gives them a list of known 1Password users, complete with names and email addresses. That list can be used to send targeted phishing emails: fake messages that pretend to be from 1Password and try to trick people into clicking a link, sharing their Secret Key or handing over other details. 1Password has warned its users to watch out for unexpected invitations, account recovery requests and other suspicious messages, and to remember that the company will never contact them to ask for their account password or Secret Key. The stolen invitation and recovery tokens cannot unlock an account on their own, the company says, because finishing that process also needs either a company sign-in or a verification code sent to the user’s email. As a precaution, 1Password has made those codes harder to guess, lengthening them from six numbers to ten letters and numbers.

The breach is the latest in a wave of cyberattacks that have hit American institutions in recent weeks. Only yesterday, this website reported that hackers had broken into an unclassified system used by the CIA and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence. Days earlier, hackers broke into the FBI’s National Crime Information Center, the database American police use to share records on fugitives and arrest warrants. And only last week, the US Treasury’s Financial Crimes Enforcement Network had to take its filing systems offline after unauthorised access. At the same time, security researchers disclosed a critical flaw in the Linux operating system — a remote root hole in its file-sharing server that has existed since 2014 and affects millions of computers. Taken together, the incidents show that even the best-protected organisations, from spy agencies to the companies that guard our passwords, are under constant attack.

1Password says it has hired independent cybersecurity experts to check its clean-up work and confirm that the fixes are solid. It is now notifying the customers whose information was taken, and it says it has found no evidence that any customer account has been taken over. The company is not asking users to change their passwords or Secret Keys. For ordinary users, the advice is simple: do not click on links in emails about this breach, open the 1Password app or website directly instead, and be extra careful with any message that claims your account needs attention. If a message asks for your Secret Key, it is a scam — delete it. The incident is also a reminder for every company: a single employee account, in this case on GitHub, can become the key that opens the door to the whole system — which is why strong two-step verification on every work account matters so much.

Questions people ask

Were my passwords stolen in the 1Password breach? No. 1Password says no vault content, passwords or encryption keys were accessed. Your saved logins stay locked because only you hold the keys that can open them.

Do I need to change my master password or Secret Key? No, the company says you do not need to update anything in your 1Password account. But stay alert for phishing emails that pretend to be about this breach.

What information did the hackers actually take? Names, sign-in email addresses, account and user IDs, account creation and last sign-in times, public keys, some sign-in details, and tokens from invitation and recovery email links — for a small subset of customers.

How did the hackers get in? Through an employee’s corporate GitHub account. The attacker used it to reach private source code and push malicious code into 1Password’s backend systems, which then downloaded customer account records.
